How Much Does an Interpreter Cost in China?

By Leon Lau · Updated 2 November 2026 · 7 min read

If you are planning a factory visit, a trade fair or a sourcing trip in China, the first question is almost always the same: what will the interpreter cost? The honest answer is that there is no single price — but there is a clear, predictable range, and once you know what moves it you can budget with confidence and spot a bad quote immediately.

This guide gives you the real numbers I see in the market, explains what is normally included, and shows where agencies quietly add margin. All figures are indicative 2026 rates for English–Chinese business interpreting in mainland China.

The short answer

A professional English–Chinese interpreter in China typically costs ¥800–¥2,500 per day (roughly US$110–350) for a standard 8-hour day. General business and escort work sits at the lower end; technical factory visits and trade-fair days sit higher. Beijing and Shanghai run 20–40% above Guangdong. Simultaneous interpreting is a separate, much higher category.

A useful sanity check: if a quote for a normal Guangdong factory visit comes in far below ¥800 for a full day, you are probably being offered a student, a machine-translation app with a helper, or someone who will cancel on you. If it comes in far above ¥3,000, you are likely paying an agency layer rather than the interpreter.

What actually drives the price

1. Language pair and direction

English ↔ Mandarin is the most common and therefore the most competitively priced pair. Rare pairs (e.g. Portuguese, Arabic, Turkish, Scandinavian languages) can be 50–100% more expensive because the pool of qualified interpreters in China is small.

2. Technical difficulty

A retail meeting needs vocabulary. A moulding factory, a PCB line, a chemical plant or a medical device audit needs an interpreter who understands the process and can hold a technical conversation without guessing. That specialisation is the single biggest price multiplier inside the normal range.

3. Duration and timing

Short notice (under 48 hours), weekend work, public holidays and overnight shifts all carry premiums, usually 20–50%. A job that runs 10–12 hours is billed with overtime on top of the day rate.

4. Location

Local Guangdong work is the cheapest because there is no travel time. Beijing, Shanghai and Hangzhou are more expensive on both the day rate and the logistics. Remote cities add a travel day, flights and hotel on top.

5. Who you book through

Booking the interpreter directly usually costs 30–50% less than booking through an agency for the same person, because the agency margin disappears. You also get to speak to the person who will actually stand next to you.

6. Consecutive vs simultaneous

Consecutive interpreting (the interpreter speaks after each sentence) is the default for factory and business work. Simultaneous interpreting requires a booth, headsets, two interpreters rotating every 20–30 minutes, and a technician — a completely different budget.

City differences you should budget for

  • Guangdong (Dongguan, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Foshan, Zhongshan): the most competitive market, strong supply of manufacturing-experienced interpreters.
  • Shanghai, Hangzhou, Ningbo, Nanjing: roughly 20–40% above Guangdong for equivalent experience.
  • Beijing: highest day rates, especially for conference and policy-adjacent work.
  • Chengdu, Chongqing, Xi'an: mid-range day rates, but travel logistics often add more than the rate difference.
  • Yiwu: a special case — a very deep bench of market-savvy interpreters at low day rates, but quality varies enormously.

What is usually included — and what is extra

Clarity here prevents the awkward conversation at the end of the day.

Normally included

  • Interpreting for the agreed hours
  • Preparation: reading your product notes and agenda
  • On-site coordination and basic cultural guidance
  • Note-taking and follow-up summary (common, not universal)

Normally extra

  • Travel: train, flights, taxi, fuel
  • Hotel and meals when working away from home
  • Overtime beyond 8 hours
  • Weekend / public-holiday premium
  • Booth, headsets and technician for simultaneous work

Five warning signs in a quote

  • No named interpreter. If the quote does not say who will actually attend, you may be sold a "category", not a person.
  • A price that is suspiciously low. Below-market quotes usually mean an inexperienced interpreter, a last-minute substitution, or a hidden overtime charge.
  • No written scope. Hours, location, travel, and what happens if the meeting runs late should all be in writing before you pay a deposit.
  • Vague experience claims. Ask which industries and which trade fairs. "Many years of experience" is not an answer.
  • Payment only in full, upfront, to a personal account with no contract. A deposit plus balance on completion is normal; full prepayment with no paperwork is not.

Frequently asked questions

Do interpreters in China charge per day or per hour?

Almost always per day. A standard day is 8 working hours. For a short meeting, most interpreters offer a half-day rate, usually 60–70% of the full day rate.

Is travel and accommodation included in the daily rate?

No. The day rate covers interpreting only. Travel, hotel and meals outside the local area are quoted separately, and long-distance jobs usually include one travel day each way.

Why are agency quotes sometimes double the freelance rate?

Agencies add a margin for sales, coordination, contracts and replacement cover, and they often take 30–50% of what the client pays. Booking the interpreter directly removes that layer and usually gets you the same person for less.

Do you charge extra for overtime?

Yes. Work beyond 8 hours is billed by the hour, typically at one eighth of the day rate with a minimum of one hour. Weekend and public-holiday work carries a 20–50% premium.

How far in advance should I book?

One to two weeks is comfortable for a normal factory or business visit. For major trade fairs such as Canton Fair, book three to four weeks ahead, because good interpreters are fully booked in the week before the show.

The bottom line

Budget US$110–350 per day for a professional English–Chinese interpreter in China, add travel and hotel for any job outside your city, and expect a premium for technical content, weekends and short notice. Compare quotes on who is coming and what is included, not just the number — the cheapest quote is rarely the cheapest job.
